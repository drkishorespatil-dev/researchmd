The data problem, and why it resolves cleanly

This is the part worth reading carefully.

Table 7 counted 20 patients with persistent symptoms at one month. Tables 5 and 6 both counted 21. Separately, Table 6 classified four patients whose preoperative endoscopy was normal as having “improved” on repeat endoscopy — you cannot improve on normal.

Those two faults turn out to be one fault. Table 6’s normal-endoscopy row read 4 improved / 6 persistent, which is character-for-character the symptom outcome for that same group in Table 5 — symptom data had been pasted into the endoscopy table. Once that row is corrected to what it can only be (all 10 normal, unchanged), every marginal in the study locks together:

Serious — would draw a rejection or correction

01
Critical

Sample size calculation does not compute

The Methods state n = z²pq/d² with p = 75.5%, 95% confidence and 5% absolute precision, giving 85. Run it: 1.96² × 0.755 × 0.245 ÷ 0.05² = 284.2. At 10% precision it gives 71.1, which rounds up to 72 — and 85 comfortably exceeds that.

This is the single most dangerous line in the paper. It is checkable in thirty seconds and an editor who checks it will assume the rest of the statistics were handled the same way.

Rewritten: minimum 72 at 10% precision; 85 consecutive patients enrolled, exceeding the minimum.
02
Critical

Table 7 contradicted Tables 5 and 6

20 patients with persistent symptoms against 21. The manuscript carried an internal note acknowledging this and asking for it to be resolved — that note would have shipped to the editor had it not been caught.

Reallocated to the normal-postoperative group per your instruction (9/70, 12.9%). OR recalculated to 27.11 (6.39–115.10), Fisher p = 7.0 × 10⁻⁷. Abstract, Results and legend updated; the internal note deleted.
03
Critical

Table 6 contained an impossible category

Four patients with a normal preoperative endoscopy were classified as “improved” at one month. Improvement relative to a normal baseline is not definable, and any gastroenterology reviewer will say so.

Normal-preop row corrected to 0 improved / 10 unchanged / 0 worsened. Methods now defines the categories explicitly, including that a normal examination remaining normal counts as unchanged. FFH p tightens to < 0.001.

Judgement calls that are yours, not mine.

A

Reference 17 is published in a journal Cureus reviewers may not accept

International Journal of Science and Research is not indexed in PubMed, MEDLINE, Scopus or Web of Science and appears on several caution lists. It is load-bearing in your Discussion — Maheshwari and Gond's 88.6% is your closest comparator, the one that makes your own 88.2% look expected rather than anomalous.

I would not delete it, but I would not let the argument rest on it alone. Kunnuru (75.5%) and Khedkar (76.25%) are already carrying that paragraph.

B

Reference 18 has a year that disagrees with its DOI

Saudi Surg J 2020, 8:185–91, DOI suffix ssj_84_21. The DOI vintage suggests 2021. Check the volume year against the publisher record — Cureus validates DOIs on submission.

C

Reference 13 is 27 years old and supports a present-tense claim

Romshoo et al. 1998 is cited twice for the current burden of H. pylori in India. The claim is almost certainly still true, but a 1998 Kashmir series is thin support for a statement about present-day national prevalence, and it is exactly the sort of thing a reviewer picks at when the whole paper turns on regional prevalence.

D

t-tests with ten patients in one arm

Independent-samples t-tests compared age and symptom duration between endoscopy groups where one group has n = 10. Not wrong, and both comparisons are resoundingly null (p = 0.965, 0.855), so the conclusion will not move. But expect a reviewer to ask for Mann-Whitney U. Worth having it run in advance.

E

Symptom outcome and endoscopic outcome agree perfectly

In the abnormal-preoperative group, 60 improved endoscopically and 60 became symptom-free; 15 did not improve and 15 stayed symptomatic. Perfect concordance is biologically plausible here, but it is unusual enough that a careful reviewer will ask whether the two outcomes were assessed independently or whether one informed the other. Have the patient-level cross-tab ready.

Before you submit

Five items. The document flags each one in red where it sits.

  • Verify the Table 6 and Table 7 reconciliation against your master chart. The reconstruction is internally consistent across all seven tables, but it is a reconstruction. This is the one item that must not be skipped.
  • Confirm the endoscope. Olympus, Tokyo, Japan is filled in as the likely make — check your unit records.
  • Supply operative details. Exact number and nature of intraoperative complications, and mean (SD) postoperative stay in days.
  • Supply the ethics approval reference number and date.
  • Attach the three endoscopy PNGs at the marked positions, with patient identifiers removed from the image frames.

Then delete every red author query, restore the author table and contributions in an unblinded copy, and it is ready to go.
